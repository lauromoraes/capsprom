\chapter{Experimental Protocol}\label{chap:experimental-protocol}

\section{Experiment Configuration}\label{sec:protocol-configuration}

Each experiment is specified by a tuple
$(\mathcal{D},\Phi,f,\theta,E,r)$, where $\mathcal{D}$ is the labeled dataset,
$\Phi$ is the combined feature representation, $f$ is the classifier or neural
architecture, $\theta$ is the set of training and model parameters, $E$ is the
set of evaluation outcomes, and $r$ denotes the random seed or replication
index. The configuration, dataset hashes, software environment, and resulting
artifacts are recorded through MLflow so that every reported value is traceable
to a versioned YAML specification.

The protocol distinguishes development from confirmatory evaluation. The
deduplicated Bhandari human shuffle-control split is the initial development
benchmark. Its negative examples are deterministic mononucleotide shuffles and
therefore test discrimination of promoter sequence order under controlled base
composition, not discrimination from genomic non-promoters. Because its test
results have already been observed during pipeline development, they are
reported as fixed-split reference results rather than as blind confirmatory
evidence. The contaminated legacy Bhandari partition is retained only for
provenance and historical comparability. Confirmatory comparisons use the 18
eligible strict-DNA CDBProm and CNNProm datasets whose positive and negative
class construction passed source-semantic review, with model specifications and search spaces
frozen before test predictions are generated. Their legacy fixed partitions do
not satisfy the integrity criteria and are replaced by deduplicated group-safe
partitions before confirmatory use. Dataset-level results are the
primary units of cross-dataset inference, preventing the largest sources from
dominating the overall conclusion.

\section{Data Integrity and Model Selection}\label{sec:protocol-integrity}

Each train--test pair is audited for strict DNA compliance, class counts, exact
duplicates, conflicting labels, normalized partition overlap, and available
source groups. Immediately after cropping and before partitioning, every
normalized sequence observed with both labels is removed in full from the
positive and negative classes. No label is inferred or preferred. Repeated
same-label identities are collapsed to one row, strict-DNA filtering is applied,
and unique exact-sequence groups are assigned deterministically to train or
test. A filtering manifest records, for each dataset, the number of rows
entering, removed from, and remaining after cropping, collision removal,
within-class deduplication, strict-DNA filtering, and partitioning. Fixed
benchmark partitions are retained for comparability when
they pass the declared integrity criteria. A contaminated partition is retained
only as a labeled legacy analysis and is replaced by a group-safe split for the
primary development workflow, as in the Bhandari human correction. When
biological or source groups are available, resampling preserves those groups.

Eligibility is evaluated after all declared filters. A confirmatory pair must
contain both classes in both partitions, no same-label exact duplicate rows, no
cross-label collisions, and no exact sequence shared by training and test.
It must additionally have a documented, biologically defensible construction
for both labels and must not use strand or another metadata field as an
unverified proxy for promoter status. This additional gate excludes the eight
GraphPro datasets from the confirmatory scope even though their cleaned v3
tables pass exact-identity checks. Their historical results are exploratory.
Failure of any condition blocks confirmatory use but does not erase the source
artifact: the original hashes, counts, reason for exclusion, correction policy,
and corrected hashes remain in the audit trail. Near-duplicate and homology
analyses are reported separately and do not replace these exact-identity gates.

Coordinate-based reconstruction additionally requires agreement between the
primary publication, accession version, and extracted sequence. A coordinate
table is not accepted merely because its row count or organism label appears
plausible. For each source, a frozen row sample is compared by position and
strand, and any sequence supplied by the publication is recreated from the
declared reference. Failure of the accession check invalidates all derived
windows, controls, partitions, and results for that candidate; it is not treated
as a removable duplicate or corrected by renaming an already extracted file.

All data-dependent preprocessing and feature selection are fitted only on the
training partition. Hyperparameters are selected using one fixed five-fold
stratified training manifest generated with seed 42. Models in a comparison
receive an explicitly comparable search budget. Test outcomes are not used for
feature selection, early stopping, threshold selection, calibration, or
hyperparameter optimization.

\section{Data Conditions and Comparability}\label{sec:protocol-data-conditions}

Two data conditions are distinguished, because they answer different
questions. In the \emph{released} condition, a dataset is derived from the files
distributed by a source study, whose sequences and labels are kept unchanged;
it answers what the published benchmark supports. In the \emph{reconstructed}
condition, the examples are rebuilt from independently verified references,
such as a genome assembly and a dated transcription start site annotation; it
answers what holds when the biological identity of each example has been
established without relying on the released files. Results of the two
conditions are reported separately and never pooled.

Every change between an acquired file and an experimental dataset is assigned
to one of three classes, tested in a fixed order. A change is a
\emph{reconstruction} when any row comes to depend on data other than its
released record, for instance a window re-extracted from a genome or a label
not present in the release. It is a \emph{metadata correction} when only
descriptive fields, such as the organism or the assembly, change, the rows,
sequences, and labels remain identical, and the correction rests on a verified
finding; the value declared by the source is kept beside the corrected one. It
is a \emph{filtering} step when whole records are removed by a rule declared
before the dataset is built. A dataset is labelled as released only if every
row can be traced to an acquired record through filtering or a protocol
transformation applied to all records alike; otherwise it is reconstructed.
A modified dataset is therefore never presented as an original one by default.

Within the released condition, two protocols are separated. The
\emph{published} protocol repeats the split, window, negative generation,
subsampling, and metrics of the source study as far as they are documented,
and is used only to reproduce its reported baselines. The \emph{common}
protocol is the one defined in this chapter and is used to compare models
across sources. Each result states its comparability with the published value:
direct, when the published protocol is applied to the released data with the
filters the publication itself describes; qualified, when undocumented
parameters had to be fixed or additional filters applied; and not comparable,
when the data or the protocol differ in a way that invalidates the published
value as a reference, for example a different organism or split design. Results
of the common protocol and of the reconstructed condition are not comparable
with published values by construction. Section~\ref{sec:methods-data-acquisition}
describes a case in which this distinction decides how a released collection
may be used.

\section{Negative-Control Conditions}\label{sec:protocol-negative-controls}

Negative-set construction is treated as an explicit experimental factor rather
than as an interchangeable preprocessing choice. This distinction is required
because negative selection can materially alter both measured performance and
the shortcuts available to a sequence classifier
\citep{gonzalez2025negative,krutzfeldt2020impact}. Five conditions are
predeclared. Source-native negatives support reproduction of eligible source
studies. GC-matched genomic controls pair each verified TSS with one window from
the same assembly under frozen exclusion, identity, and GC-caliper rules.
Deterministic mono- or dinucleotide shuffles are mechanistic controls and do not
represent genomic non-promoters. CDS-derived controls form a separate coding-
background sensitivity condition. Finally, multi-negative training exposes a
model to more than one negative family without redefining any family as ground
truth.

In the multi-negative condition, each positive is represented once logically:
positives receive one half of the effective batch or loss weight, and the other
half is divided equally among the available negative families. Duplicating a
positive once for each family is prohibited. Validation and test sets remain
separate panels for source-native, genomic, shuffled, and CDS-derived controls;
their records are never concatenated to calculate one MCC. Consequently,
paired prediction-level comparisons are made only within an identical test
panel. Effects across control policies are summarized by dataset and seed and
then by the dependency clusters defined in
Section~\ref{sec:protocol-statistics}; model-by-policy interactions are
exploratory until a successor contract establishes sufficient independent
clusters.

All sequences connected by a TSS, positive identity, source experiment,
positive--control pair, shuffle parent, or near-sequence component share one
global partition group across control conditions. Control construction and its
parameters use no held-out outcomes. Before real generation or training, the
protocol requires completion of the GraphPro source audit, validation of the
statistical aggregation fixtures, a coordinate-and-assembly feasibility
inventory, and prospective freezing of one small pilot dataset. Thus the
current contract defines the comparisons but does not yet authorize their
execution.

\section{Replication and Prediction Contract}\label{sec:protocol-replication}

Stochastic models are trained with ten predeclared seeds. The fixed seed set is
shown below so that it can be reproduced independently of the implementation:
\[
  \{13,29,42,71,101,137,173,211,251,293\}.
\]
Every stochastic component receives the run seed. Deterministic models are
fitted once per locked configuration and are not repeated to create
pseudo-replicates. Stochastic dataset-level results are summarized primarily by
their median and interquartile range, with mean and standard deviation retained
as supplementary descriptors.

The folds and seeds have distinct inferential roles. Inner folds are used only
for model and epoch selection and are not treated as independent test
observations. Seeds quantify variation caused by initialization, sampling, and
training order conditional on the same dataset; consequently, they are not
independent biological replications. Stochastic models are paired by the same
seed identifier, while a deterministic model is fitted once and is not repeated
artificially. These restrictions avoid the optimistic tests that can result
from treating correlated cross-validation estimates as independent
observations~\cite{dietterich1998approximate}.

Every test record receives a positive-class score and a binary prediction. The
primary decision threshold is 0.5. A threshold selected exclusively from
out-of-fold training predictions may be reported as a secondary analysis but is
frozen before test evaluation. Sigmoid or isotonic calibration is likewise fit
only from training-derived out-of-fold predictions; raw and calibrated models
are treated as separate locked conditions.

\section{Evaluation Outcomes}\label{sec:protocol-metrics}

Matthews correlation coefficient (MCC) is the single primary effectiveness
outcome because it incorporates every confusion-matrix cell and remains
informative under class imbalance. Table~\ref{tab:protocol-outcomes} summarizes
the outcome families used to characterize effectiveness, calibration,
efficiency, and robustness. Secondary metric inference is interpreted as
exploratory so that multiple correlated measures do not inflate claims of
superiority.

Efficiency timing uses one untimed warm-up followed by three timed executions

are reported together with peak resident memory, software versions, processor
or accelerator identity, thread count, and input size.

\section{Uncertainty and Statistical Comparison}\label{sec:protocol-statistics}

For each dataset and locked run, 95\% confidence intervals are estimated from
10,000 stratified bootstrap resamples of the test records. The same resampled
indices are applied to every model in a pair. A group bootstrap replaces
record-level resampling when defensible biological or source groups are
available; otherwise, the possible dependence between sequences is stated as a
limitation.

For models $A$ and $B$, the seed-level effect in dataset $d$ and seed $s$ is
$\Delta_{d,s}=\mathrm{MCC}_{A,d,s}-\mathrm{MCC}_{B,d,s}$. The dataset-level
effect is $\Delta_d=\operatorname{median}_s(\Delta_{d,s})$, that is, the median
of paired seed differences rather than the difference between independently
aggregated model summaries. A deterministic baseline contributes one locked
MCC, which is subtracted from every stochastic-seed result without turning
those repeated differences into independent observations.

Within one dataset, the principal output is the paired MCC effect and its
paired-bootstrap interval. Each bootstrap resample applies the same record or
biological-group indices to every model and seed and recomputes the complete
seed-aggregated effect. Paired randomization and McNemar's exact test may be
reported per locked seed as secondary analyses of prediction disagreement.

Before cross-dataset inference, a dependency manifest records source,
organism, biological domain, derivation lineage, negative-generation method,
and a justified dependency-cluster identifier. Related datasets derived from
the same source pool contribute one cluster-level median effect, preventing
dataset variants from inflating the effective sample size. Across at least ten
audited independent clusters, a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test evaluates
two-model differences. For three or more models, a Friedman omnibus test is
followed only by predeclared pairwise Wilcoxon comparisons
~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Median effects and win--tie--loss counts accompany
all tests.

The family-wise significance level is 0.05. Holm correction is applied across
confirmatory model-pair hypotheses for the primary outcome. Conclusions
emphasize effect sizes and uncertainty; statistical significance alone is not
interpreted as biological importance.

\section{Reproducibility and Reporting}\label{sec:protocol-controls}

Each MLflow run records the protocol version, dataset and fold-manifest hashes,
feature and model definitions, seed, software and hardware metadata, row-level
test predictions, effectiveness and calibration outcomes, timing and memory
measurements, resampling seeds, comparison outputs, and fitted artifacts.
Prediction files use stable row identifiers without unnecessarily reproducing
raw sequences.

Reports label analyses as exploratory, developmental, or confirmatory and state
dataset eligibility, exclusions, failed runs, replication counts, and the
resampling unit. The complete machine-readable contract is maintained in
\path{configs/evaluation_protocol_v3.yaml}, while implementation requirements and
limitations are maintained in
\path{specs/statistical_evaluation_protocol.md}.
