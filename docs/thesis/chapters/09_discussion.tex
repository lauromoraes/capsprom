\chapter{Discussion}\label{chap:discussion}

\section{Interpretation of Results}\label{sec:discussion-interpretation}

The corrected Bhandari development experiment shows that the baseline models
can distinguish authentic promoter windows from composition-preserving shuffled
versions without relying on exact train--test duplication. XGBoost produced the
stronger fixed-seed result, suggesting that nonlinear interactions among 3-mer
composition and GC descriptors contribute to this controlled discrimination.
This finding concerns sequence-order disruption and does not establish the
ability to reject genomic non-promoter regions.

The historical canonical-v3 source-negative comparison addresses this
limitation more directly but is exploratory because it includes GraphPro.
After ten-seed replication, the median paired MCC advantage of XGBoost over
logistic regression was 0.0574 across 25 datasets, with a bootstrap 95\%
interval excluding zero and 21 dataset-level wins. The median
within-dataset standard deviation of 0.0124 shows that stochastic variation was
small relative to the cross-dataset paired effect. This supports XGBoost as the
classical reference for subsequent CapsProm comparisons, but does not establish
that either baseline transfers across organisms or source studies.

\section{Comparison With Prior Work}\label{sec:discussion-prior-work}

Results should distinguish the basic two-output-capsule CapsProm from the
qualification-stage legacy CapsProm-1C reproduction and compare both with the
physicochemical-property studies, as well as external baselines such as CNNProm
and GraphPro when reproduced under compatible conditions.

\section{Threats to Validity}\label{sec:discussion-threats}

The most immediate threat is negative-set construction. The downloaded
Bhandari collections contain promoters only, so their shuffled negatives do not
represent intergenic regions, coding regions, or other genomic backgrounds that
may generate false positives in practical use. Claims about biological
generalization must therefore rely on datasets whose negative construction is
both documented and audited. The revised confirmatory scope uses CDBProm and
CNNProm; GraphPro remains exploratory. Their initially processed
partitions were not suitable for that purpose: all 26 audited pairs contained
exact train--test overlap, covering 98.09\% of the combined test rows. This
dominant contamination was traced to append-mode writes during
legacy cropping rather than to the downloaded record multiplicities. Rebuilding
with overwrite semantics reduced overlap to 2.17\% and yielded 9 immediately
eligible pairs before collision treatment. Mandatory bilateral removal of the
1,348 post-cropping cross-label collisions removed 2,696 rows, eliminated label
conflicts, and increased the eligible count to 10. Their source-provided class
assignments remain useful for provenance but require the later semantic gate.
The subsequent canonical build removed 301
positive and 759 negative same-label duplicate rows and assigned unique
sequence groups deterministically, after which all 26 pairs passed the exact
duplicate, label-conflict, and train--test-overlap criteria. Near-duplicate and
homology dependence nevertheless remain to be evaluated before confirmatory
model comparisons are interpreted.

The Hamming-distance sensitivity audit narrows this residual concern. Only 96
of 35,991 test sequences have a training neighbor within eight substitutions,
and every detected neighbor relation is label-consistent. This low prevalence
does not justify changing the frozen primary v3 split on the basis of test-set
inspection. Instead, these rows define a secondary sensitivity analysis: model
conclusions should be recomputed after excluding affected test rows or under a
separately generated similarity-clustered split. More distant evolutionary or
functional homology is not captured by Hamming distance and remains a threat to
external validity.

The concentration of 1,345 collisions in the GraphPro \textit{E. coli}
dataset is qualitatively different from a cropping-induced collision. Every
affected raw record is duplicated across the supplied positive and negative
files with the same sequence and complete FASTA header, including coordinate
and strand. This evidence locates the contradiction in the distributed source
classes, although the available files do not establish whether it arose from
dataset packaging, negative-set construction, or an intentional source-study
choice. Bilateral exclusion is consequently more defensible than selecting one
label: relabeling would introduce an unsupported biological judgment, whereas
retaining both labels would make the supervised target internally inconsistent.
The removal is substantial---53.39\% of positive and 34.72\% of negative
records---and must therefore be considered when interpreting comparability with
the source study.

The broader GraphPro source audit shows why bilateral collision removal is
necessary but insufficient. Five bacterial folders associate every positive
record with the plus strand and every negative record with the minus strand.
Moreover, 535 GraphPro Bacillus negatives exactly match the canonical TSS
windows of CDBProm positives, and the GraphPro \texttt{Cpne} folder was
misidentified locally despite headers for \textit{Chlamydophila pneumoniae}.
These observations materially threaten construct validity: a classifier could
learn strand-associated composition or artifacts of dataset assembly rather
than promoter status. Exclusion is preferable to automatic relabeling because
neither sequence agreement nor model behavior establishes the true biological
class. The finding is itself a methodological result of the thesis, showing
that leakage-free partitions may still encode an invalid supervised target.

The independent reconstruction exercise provides a defensible route to recover
coverage without claiming to repair the released labels, but it also exposed
why accession validation must precede sequence generation. Five nominal
bacterial identities retain candidate TSS evidence; the sixth, E. coli, mixed
pAA plasmid coordinates with an MG1655 chromosome label. Provisional genomic
control generation had yielded 11,903 eligible TSS pairs and 11,826 pairs after
strand-invariant sequence deduplication. The resulting
datasets would answer a newly specified promoter-versus-matched-genomic-control
question and must therefore carry new identities rather than being presented as
the original GraphPro benchmark. Negative selection remains a design factor:
GC matching reduces an obvious composition shortcut but does not prove that a
random genomic window is biologically inactive, while CDS controls define a
different background. Reporting both as separate, predeclared conditions is
more informative than pooling them or choosing the condition with the better
MCC.

The similarity-aware partitioning result reduces a second threat: closely
related windows cannot be divided between training and testing merely because
their strings are not identical. Grouping connected components through eight
substitutions produced no cross-partition neighbor at that threshold while
preserving all 11,826 reconstructed pairs. This is strong evidence against
within-dataset sequence leakage under the declared metric, but it is not
evidence that the underlying TSS annotations or sampled genomic controls are
biologically correct. Those source and distribution gates remain logically
separate from computational cleanliness.

The formal distribution audit further shows that a pairwise tolerance does not
guarantee indistinguishable aggregate backgrounds. All bacterial controls meet
the four-base GC rule, yet small systematic class shifts remain in five
organisms. These effects are much smaller than the approximately ten to eleven
percentage-point GC separation between the released Human and Mouse class
folders, but both findings could support composition-based shortcuts. A future
sensitivity contract should therefore predeclare stricter or distribution-level
matching rather than selecting a control policy from predictive performance.

The resulting protocol therefore treats negative construction as a controlled
factor rather than selecting one universally ``best'' negative set. Models
trained on source-native, GC-matched genomic, or mixed native--genomic exposure
will be evaluated on separate native and genomic test panels. Shuffle- and
CDS-based panels remain explicitly labeled sensitivity analyses. A metric is
never pooled across these panels, because doing so would confound predictive
performance with panel composition. Stability across defensible conditions is
the relevant robustness evidence; a high MCC against only one conveniently
separable negative family is not sufficient evidence of genomic promoter
detection. This design is consistent with empirical reports that negative-set
choice materially changes regulatory-sequence predictors
\citep{gonzalez2025negative,krutzfeldt2020impact}.

Cross-dataset identity is also a design dependency rather than a cleaning
detail. The 404 shared Chlamydophila positives show that CDBProm and the
reconstruction cannot contribute independent dataset-level evidence. The six
nominal reconstructed E.~coli positives previously found among CNNProm
negatives are no longer evidence of a biological annotation disagreement: they
were extracted from the wrong reference molecule and are invalid. Automatic
deletion or relabeling would still be inappropriate; the valid remedy is a new
pAA-specific reconstruction with fresh provenance and dependencies.

The complete EPD resolution of the eukaryotic identifiers sharpens the same
distinction. All released Human identifiers and 1,116 Mouse identifiers occur
in both class files even though EPDnewNC annotates them as promoter/TSS
identities. The identifiers can anchor a new positive set, but cannot establish
that the released sequence paired with the negative directory is a biological
non-promoter. Reinstatement therefore requires recreation from hg38/mm10 and
either treated, fully logged historical controls or newly sampled genomic
controls rather than indiscriminate row deletion.

Full assembly-based recreation shows that the eukaryotic issue is not sequence
corruption introduced by CapsProm preprocessing. GraphPro systematically used
the 81 bases ending at the EPD TSS for positives and an 81-base window beginning
1 kb downstream for negatives. This makes the original benchmark reproducible,
but it changes the scientific interpretation: downstream sequence is a
constructed proxy control, not direct evidence of promoter absence. Its strong
GC separation and occasional proximity to another annotated TSS justify
reporting it separately from the primary GC-matched genomic-control condition.
The formal 100-nt TSS exclusion removes these known annotation conflicts while
preserving the slight resulting class imbalance. Deleting additional valid
positives merely to force equal counts would conflate integrity correction with
an experimental sampling decision; any balancing or class weighting must be
predeclared at training time.

The post-treatment audit also demonstrates that integrity filtering and
distribution control answer different questions. The Human and Mouse GC gaps
remain approximately 11.5 and 10.7 percentage points, with marked positional
composition differences. Consequently, the treated downstream condition is
useful for reproducibility and sensitivity analysis but should not be promoted
to the primary genomic-control condition. The reverse-complement identity
between one Mouse control and a CNNProm Mouse non-TATA positive is a separate
annotation dependency. Its version-2 exclusion is justified narrowly by control
suitability: a sequence already labeled positive in the active comparison scope
is not retained as a GraphPro negative control. This does not adjudicate the
biological label, does not alter CNNProm, and does not remove the need for a
dependency-aware protocol. The virtually unchanged GC gap after exclusion
confirms that resolving one exact conflict does not resolve the broader control
construction bias.

The legacy Bhandari split also contained extensive exact duplication: 49.87\%
of its test rows occurred as sequences in training. Although the corrected split
eliminates exact overlap, near-duplicate or homologous dependence remains a
possible threat and requires a later sensitivity analysis. Additional threats
include class imbalance, organism-specific signals, fixed-window assumptions,
heterogeneous source definitions, and differences between original and
reproduced baselines.
